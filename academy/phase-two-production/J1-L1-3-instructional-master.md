## Guided instruction — four full chapters

### Chapter 1 — Choose the support role, not the most impressive tool
Think again about the need you selected in Lesson 1.2. You identified who is affected, the barrier you suspect, and what evidence would help you establish whether the need is real. The next temptation is to ask which AI product to install. Today, we will ask a more useful question: what specific kind of support would actually help with this task?

AI can organize an approved set of notes. It can generate neutral questions before an interview. It can compare choices against criteria you provide. It can prepare a first draft, help edit for clarity, or serve as a rehearsal partner for a difficult conversation. Each is a different role, and each needs different oversight. One request may benefit from AI as an organizer; another might need a comparison partner. A third might require no AI at all.

Consider the business owner who needs to send a proposal. Sorting existing requirements is an organizing task. Developing questions for unclear requirements is question generation. Comparing two permitted delivery approaches is comparison support. Producing a first draft is draft assistance. Fixing tone and plain language is editing. Practicing how to explain a limitation to a customer is rehearsal. But authorizing a price, a delivery date, or a promise to a customer remains human responsibility.

The phrase no AI is not a failure of imagination. It is a professional option. If the job requires checking one known number against an approved record, the direct check may be simpler, faster, and more reliable than sending the number through an AI assistant. If information is sensitive and your organization has not authorized an AI system, do not send the information to one just to complete an exercise.

Make five entries in your own project. Each entry should describe a real or proposed task, the person who relies on its outcome, the potential AI role, the human decision, and the result that would matter. At least one should consider a narrow AI role or no AI.

Pause for thirty seconds and look at your list. Have you chosen roles because they fit the work, or because you feel pressure to use the technology? Professional direction begins with that distinction.

### Chapter 2 — Distinguish activity, output, usefulness, and value
Suppose a team uses AI to produce ten customer messages. We can count the ten messages. That tells us what was produced, but not whether the messages were correct or whether anyone needed them.

Let us separate four ideas. Activity means work happened: a prompt was entered, documents were compared, or a report was generated. Output means something now exists: a draft, a table, or a message. Usefulness asks whether that output helps a particular person accomplish their task. Value asks whether the work improves a relevant result enough to justify the full effort, including review, corrections, tool cost, and any work transferred to someone else.

That is a stronger test than how many words AI produces, how fast the first draft appears, or how confident the answer sounds.

In our fictional proposal example, a business owner formerly wrote the entire proposal without AI. Today an AI draft takes twenty fewer minutes to produce. That sounds encouraging. Then the owner spends thirty minutes fixing invented service promises. Compared with the previous process, ten additional minutes were consumed overall. Unless another real benefit is demonstrated, we cannot claim a net time saving. We also should not claim customers are happier or errors are lower. None of those outcomes was measured.

Perhaps AI helped the owner see missing questions earlier. That could become a useful benefit, but we would still need evidence. Perhaps a reusable, approved proposal template would work better. A sensible professional compares both approaches before investing further.

Choose a measure that reflects the need selected in Lesson 1.2. A service team could track corrections required before a customer receives an answer. A decision-making task could compare how often key evidence was missing. A communication task could look at clarity checked by an authorized reviewer. A repetitive work process might compare the total time to finish, including verification and repairs.

Record the baseline—how the work performs without the change—before claiming an improvement. If you do not have measured data, state that your numbers are estimates or proposed measures. Never invent a measured baseline to make a proposal look convincing.

### Chapter 3 — Test an AI option against a simple alternative
People sometimes evaluate an AI-assisted approach against doing nothing. That can exaggerate the apparent benefit. The fairer question is: what is the simplest realistic alternative that could solve the problem?

Return to our fictional proposal owner. Option A uses AI to draft the proposal, then requires a person to inspect all figures, approved promises, delivery terms, and omissions. Option B uses a reviewed proposal template with clearly approved descriptions and a manual check of customer-specific details.

Both options may have value. Both require a human accountable for the final proposal. An attractive first draft does not automatically make Option A the better choice. The business should compare total time, correction frequency, risk of unsupported claims, maintenance effort, and usefulness to the recipient.

If you already have reliable records, use them carefully. If you lack them, design a small, authorized test rather than inventing results. For example, use the same fictional inputs to prepare a proposal in both ways. Track time from beginning through review, record the number of important corrections, and ask an appropriate reviewer whether the final drafts meet the stated requirements. Fictional practice results must stay labeled as fictional.

You can ask your chosen AI assistant to identify roles and compare the two methods, but give it the evidence limits first. Ask it to separate supplied facts from likely benefits and unknowns. It should include the no-AI approach without dismissing it and should not guarantee that either approach saves time.

A professional experiment should be modest and useful: one task, one comparable alternative, one transparent baseline, one measure, and one person responsible for deciding whether the result is good enough.

Pause now. Of your five tasks, select one where you suspect AI could help and one where it should have a narrow role or no role. Write one sentence explaining the difference.

### Chapter 4 — Make the business value hypothesis accountable
We have chosen roles, distinguished output from value, and compared alternatives. Now we can write a value hypothesis—not an advertisement and not a promise.

A useful value hypothesis states: for this specific person and task, this proposed support may improve this particular result, compared with this baseline, as tested by this method. It also identifies who checks the work, which limitations remain, and what result would cause the team to stop or reconsider.

Here is an example for our fictional proposal owner. "If we use an AI assistant only to organize approved requirements, and keep customer promises in an approved template, we may reduce rework. We will compare the total time and number of unsupported statements across several equivalent practice proposals. The owner will verify every promise and make the final decision. We will discontinue the AI step if corrections or review effort outweigh usefulness." Notice how little is guaranteed. That is the strength of the statement.

A good value hypothesis connects to a business consequence. If the selected need is a clearer customer answer, measure whether the answer is accurate and easier to act on, not just whether AI generated it. If the need is a more dependable decision, measure the completeness of evidence and the corrections required, not the number of pages produced. If the need is less repetitive work, measure end-to-end effort, including human review and any follow-up burden.

Now open your own AI assistant on a phone, tablet, or computer. Use the approved request in this lesson, along with only fictional or sanitized notes. Ask it to classify your five tasks, state the human decision for each, compare an AI approach with a simpler non-AI alternative, identify added review effort, and show uncertainty without guaranteed savings.

Do not copy its recommendations directly into your project. Look for invented assumptions, measures without baselines, and tasks it assigned to AI even though a human must authorize the outcome. Correct those points. Choose a narrow or no-AI task and explain why. Then save your AI-role table and your evidence-labeled value hypothesis in the continuing Project Record you began in Lesson 1.1.

Your progress is not measured by how much work you give AI. It is measured by your ability to direct appropriate support and recognize real value. Keep the evidence. Keep the decision human.
