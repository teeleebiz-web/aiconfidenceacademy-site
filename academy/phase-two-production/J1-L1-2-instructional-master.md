## Instructor introduction — recording script
Welcome back to the AI Confidence Academy. In the previous lesson, you practiced taking responsibility for the work AI helps you produce. You checked the facts before accepting a polished report. Today, we go one step earlier. Before we ask AI to build something, we ask whether we understand the actual need.

Imagine someone says, “We need a chatbot.” A chatbot might help. But why do they need it? Are customers missing information? Are employees using different instructions? Are questions reaching the wrong person? Those are different problems, and they may need different solutions.

Today, you'll learn to separate a visible symptom from its possible cause. You'll compare three needs, investigate the evidence, and use your own AI assistant to prepare fair questions rather than invent answers. By the end, you'll select one provisional need for the project you began in Lesson 1.1. Your decision will be open to correction when better evidence arrives.

You bring the purpose and judgment. AI can help you organize what you know and ask what you still need to learn. Let's begin with the person who needs the work to improve.

## Guided instruction — four full chapters

### Chapter 1 — Begin with a person, not a product
Suppose you work with a small customer-service team. Every week someone says, “We spend too much time answering the same questions.” It's tempting to name an AI product right away. Perhaps you ask an assistant to create a chatbot. A few minutes later, you have a convincing plan, with software features, a launch schedule, and a promise that customers will be happier.

But a plan can be very organized without being pointed at the correct problem.

The repeated questions are what the team notices. We call that a **symptom**. A symptom matters. It tells us something in the work is difficult. But it isn't yet an explanation of why. The possible reason might be that the product information is hard to find. Maybe an outdated instruction is still being used. Maybe requests arrive in the wrong department. A different cause changes what a useful solution looks like.

Now move the camera away from the software and toward a person. Think about a customer trying to learn the next step after submitting a request. Where are they? What are they trying to accomplish? What prevents them from doing it? What would a useful result make possible? You don't need to know the answer yet. You need questions that lead to it.

Here is the Audience-Need View we will use today. Start with the **person**. Add the **situation** in which the need appears. State the **need** in language that does not prescribe a tool. Identify the **barrier** that may be getting in the way. Describe the **desired result** in ordinary terms.

For example: “A customer who submitted a service request needs to find the current next-step instruction, but the approved information is scattered across three pages. A useful result would let that customer find an accurate answer without being redirected.” Even this statement is provisional until we've checked the evidence behind the claim about three pages. It is not enough for an AI model to phrase it well.

Open the project record you started in Lesson 1.1. Read the person served and the recurring task. Ask whether those statements describe the need or jump straight to a proposed tool. If you wrote “build an AI assistant for staff,” try instead: “help staff provide consistent answers using approved information.” Keep both versions in your notes so you can see the difference.

Pause here and write one sentence naming the person and the result they need. Don't name software in that sentence.

### Chapter 2 — The symptom and the possible causes
Let's examine the fictional support desk in greater detail. A supervisor has noticed that staff answer the same product question repeatedly. That is the symptom. The supervisor believes the team needs a chatbot.

Before agreeing, consider three possible explanations.

First, the current answers might exist, but customers cannot find them. If that is true, the least complicated response might be a more visible reference page with clear instructions. Second, staff members may be reading different versions of the policy. If that is the cause, adding a chatbot on top of inconsistent policy could spread inconsistency faster. The responsible first step might be to reconcile and approve a single reference. Third, requests could be reaching the wrong team. If the problem is routing, changing the routing process may help more than generating new answers.

One symptom. Three hypotheses. None proven simply because someone wrote them on a screen.

An AI model can help generate competing explanations. That's useful when we need to see beyond our first assumption. But a suggested cause is not an observed cause. Ask yourself what record or conversation could distinguish among the possibilities. A current help-page audit might show missing information. A comparison of the written policies might show conflicting guidance. A request-routing sample might show repeated misassignment. We should not invent what any of these checks would reveal.

There is another trap. A team member may say, “Customers hate our system.” That is a reported experience, and perhaps an important one. But it is not yet a measured statement about every customer. We can preserve the speaker's concern accurately: “A team member reported repeated customer frustration.” Then we decide what additional evidence would confirm its scale, frequency, or cause.

When you compare needs in your project, keep the symptom and the cause in separate fields. Mark a possible explanation as “hypothesis” until the evidence supports it. The purpose is not to make the problem sound serious. It is to find the problem worth solving.

### Chapter 3 — What counts as evidence?
Evidence comes in different forms, and professional judgment includes knowing what kind you actually have.

An **observed fact** may be an approved activity record or a direct inspection you performed. A **reported experience** comes from a person describing what happened. An **assumption** is a proposition you have not established. A **simulation** is invented for practice, like the support-desk example we're using. Public or authorized research can also be useful, but its relevance and freshness still need checking.

These categories are not interchangeable. A fictional report with 42 requests and 36 marked resolved can help us practice verification. It cannot prove that a real business has the same challenge. Likewise, a model that predicts customers will prefer a chatbot has generated a hypothesis, not conducted a customer interview.

Let's try a simple comparison. “The latest approved support log contains repeated instances of the same question.” That statement requires the log and a clear definition of repeated. “One staff member said the instructions are difficult to use.” That is reported testimony, not necessarily a universal fact. “The cause is the website design.” That remains a hypothesis until tested. “Five simulated customers preferred an AI answer.” Those are invented practice results and must never be represented as genuine research.

For every claim you save, ask: Where did it come from? Who is authorized to share it? Is the record current? What does it show, and what does it not show? If the evidence is incomplete, label the gap.

Now take the three candidate needs from your own work. You may not have complete evidence for any of them yet. That's acceptable. It's better to state one useful uncertainty clearly than to publish three polished unsupported claims.

### Chapter 4 — Ask fairly, then choose provisionally
An interview is useful only if the question lets the other person describe their actual experience.

Compare two questions. “Wouldn't a helpful chatbot make your work easier?” This pushes the person toward the answer you want. It includes your solution before you've confirmed the need.

Try: “When this question comes up, what do you do first?” Or: “Where do you look for the current answer?” Or: “What happens when you cannot find it?” These questions invite a description of the current process. They do not ask the respondent to approve your favorite software.

Here are five example neutral questions for our fictional support desk:
1. What question or request comes up most often in your work?
2. Where do you currently find the approved answer?
3. What happens when the needed information is missing or unclear?
4. Which step takes the most effort, and how do you know?
5. What would a successful resolution allow the customer or employee to do?

These are examples, not completed interviews. We have not spoken to actual staff or customers.

Now open your own AI tool. Paste the sanitized notes for three possible needs. Ask it to distinguish symptoms, possible causes and assumptions, and to suggest five neutral questions. Examine what it generates. Remove any question that presumes the problem is real, assumes a cause, or asks someone to endorse a particular tool.

Choose one need provisionally. Explain why it matters to a person, what evidence supports the choice, and what remains unknown. Then state the evidence that would change your mind. If a verified record shows the need rarely occurs, perhaps your priority should change. If a current policy already solves it, perhaps the work is making the policy easier to find rather than generating a new answer.

Finally, return to your continuing Project Record. Save the Audience-Need View for the selected need, three compared possibilities, five neutral questions, the evidence labels, and the condition that would reverse your choice.

Your accomplishment today is not that AI produced a list. It is that you made a defensible, revisable selection before committing to a solution. In professional work, that decision can save far more effort than generating another attractive plan.

## Four narrated visual demonstrations: source-to-decision case

**Fictional case invariant:** Repeated support questions are the reported symptom. The three hypotheses are hard-to-find product information, inconsistent policy, and incorrect routing. None is established. A chatbot is a possible option, never an established need. Screen labels say **FICTIONAL TRAINING EXAMPLE**. ### 01 — See the symptom
**Onscreen:** Support-desk notes: “Staff say they answer similar questions repeatedly.” Separate columns: observed record (not supplied), reported experience, and unknown frequency.
**Narration:** “The team reports repeated questions. That is a symptom, not a diagnosis. We do not yet have an approved count, a current policy audit, or a routing record. Notice what we know and what we still need. Naming a chatbot now would skip the investigation.”
**Cue:** Highlight REPORT, then UNKNOWN; leave proposed solution unselected.

### 02 — Compare three causes
**Onscreen:** Hypothesis A: instructions hard to find. B: policy versions conflict. C: questions routed incorrectly. Each has “evidence needed.”
**Narration:** “Three explanations could lead to the same repeated question. If information is hard to find, improve the reference. If policies disagree, reconcile them. If requests go to the wrong team, review routing. We have not established which cause is correct. AI is helping us compare possibilities, not delivering a verdict.”
**Cue:** Evidence-needed cells illuminate one at a time.

### 03 — Improve the questions
**Onscreen:** Leading: “Would you prefer our AI chatbot?” Replace with: “Where do you look for the current answer?” and “What happens if it is unclear?”
**Narration:** “The first question sells our solution. It does not investigate the person's experience. Better questions begin with the work as it happens: where someone looks, what interrupts the task, and what they need to do next. We can ask AI to suggest neutral questions, but we must review them before using them.”
**Cue:** Red strike-through on leading question; calm underline on neutral questions.

### 04 — Save a provisional decision
**Onscreen:** Three-need comparison; one selected provisionally; evidence field reads “Needs authorized source check”; change-my-mind condition visible. No invented interview findings.
**Narration:** “The responsible choice is provisional. Record the audience, the situation, the likely barrier and the result you hope to improve. Label unresolved evidence and say what could change your decision. A polished AI explanation does not prove the need exists. Save this reasoning in your continuing project record.”
**Cue:** Save the evidence-labeled opportunity note; no false verification check marks.

For each, list the person affected, the observed or reported symptom, possible causes clearly labeled as hypotheses, the evidence still needed, and a question that could disprove the hypothesis. Do not invent interviews or counts.

### Required completion check
Learner explains the difference between symptom and root cause, identifies at least one authentic or appropriately authorized evidence source (or transparently unresolved need for such a source), names what would change the choice, and rejects the assertion that an AI-suggested chatbot proves customer demand. The evidence note is linked to the existing project record.
