## Guided instruction — four full chapters

### Chapter 1 — Describe the work as it happens now
Welcome to Journey Two. In Journey One, you developed a professional operating model and selected one small, finishable capstone artifact. Now the work becomes practical. We are going to study the process your artifact will improve.

Before you automate a task, you need to know how the task actually happens today. That means finding the people involved, the information that starts it, the steps performed, the decisions that change its direction, the tools used, the places where work changes hands, and the person who checks the result.

Think of a customer asking a service business for an estimate. Perhaps they send an inquiry, a team member reviews it, someone notices a missing specification, another person requests clarification, the customer responds, a qualified person drafts the estimate and a manager checks the terms. A simple sentence such as "We prepare estimates" conceals all those responsibilities and dependencies.

We call the structured view of this movement the Workflow View. It covers people, inputs, steps, decisions, tools, handoffs and review. All seven dimensions matter because each can reveal a different kind of problem.

Let us also clarify four terms that are sometimes treated as interchangeable. A task is one action, such as looking up an approved price. A process organizes related actions toward a result. A workflow shows the actual movement of the work, including decision branches and handoffs. A system is broader still: it includes standards, ownership, feedback, information and continuity so the work remains reliable over time.

A map is not a decorative flowchart. It is a working explanation that a person who performs the job can recognize. If the map leaves out the person who checks policy, it is incomplete. If it shows an automatic response that does not currently exist, it is not a current-state map. If it treats an uncertain step as confirmed, it could mislead someone into making the wrong improvement.

Start with your continuing project and write one clear entry point. What request or event begins the work? What does a completed, reviewed result look like? Then record each step between those two points in the order it actually occurs, as far as your available evidence allows.

Do not create a perfect future workflow yet. Begin with what happens now. A proper improvement starts with accurate observation, not the most attractive AI diagram.

You can work from approved records, authorized conversations or your own legitimately observed activity. If you are practicing with an invented business situation, mark the entire map as fictional. The distinction will be important when we identify friction.

### Chapter 2 — Find friction without blaming the person
Friction is anything in a workflow that makes a useful result harder, slower or less dependable than it needs to be. It can appear as waiting time, repeated effort, missing information, inconsistent results or rework. It can also occur when the next step is unclear, the decision owner is missing, or a supposedly completed action has to be checked again.

Now return to the fictional estimate request. A customer provides the type of service needed, but leaves out two specifications. Nobody is responsible for requesting those details. The estimate sits for three days. A manager eventually discovers the missing information, asks for clarification and resumes the request.

Someone may say, "The estimate team is slow." That statement blames a group without establishing the cause. A better observation is that the request waited three days because a missing input had no assigned owner. But even that explanation should be labeled fictional in the exercise and checked against records in a real setting.

The delay is not at the final email stage. Automating that email might make the last minute of the process faster while the estimate still waits three days. The work would look more automated but the person's actual need would remain unresolved.

This is the difference between a visible symptom and a likely cause. In Lesson 1.2, you practiced that distinction while choosing a business need. Now you will apply it to specific handoffs in the workflow.

Look for at least three friction points in your process. One might be a missing or incomplete input. Another might be a repeat approval that adds no new information. A third might be a handoff where the receiving person has no reliable notification or owner. For each, record where it happens, who is affected, what you observed or what was reported, what cause you suspect and what evidence would confirm it.

Friction should be understood as a property of the work rather than a flaw in the person doing it. An employee who regularly has to guess which form is current may be coping with missing process ownership. A customer who repeats a detail may be responding to a confusing request. An automatic tool that silently loses important information can introduce a new point of friction instead of removing one.

A thoughtful professional interviews or observes people respectfully, checks authorized records where possible and marks uncertainty rather than inventing a diagnosis.

The question is not "Where can I add AI?" The question is "Where is the work becoming unnecessarily difficult, and what would actually remove the reason?"

### Chapter 3 — Make decisions and handoffs visible
A workflow changes at decisions. The estimate request may contain all required inputs or it may not. A reviewer may find an unsupported price claim and return the draft for correction. A customer may approve a proposal, request clarification or decide not to proceed.

Your Workflow View should make these branches visible. When all required information is present, work may move to drafting. When information is missing, a specific owner requests it and the work waits in a known state. When an estimate is ready, a human reviewer checks every relevant number and service promise against authorized information before any outward message is sent.

A handoff is a transfer of responsibility or work between people, teams or tools. Every handoff should have a sending party, a receiving party, the information transferred, the expected next action and a way to identify what happens if the handoff fails.

In our fictional estimate example, the missing-specification handoff is weak. It has no explicit owner and no trigger for follow-up. The team does not need an AI-generated estimate to fix the most immediate problem. It needs a defined check for complete inputs and an accountable person to request missing details.

AI could help organize an approved intake checklist or draft a clarification message for human review. But the authority to send it, approve a price, or accept binding terms remains with the appropriate person. The revised workflow might include a simple form or rule before it includes any AI generation.

This is why we map the current state separately from a proposed future state. The current-state map explains what really happens and why. The improved map changes a specific step or handoff for a reason we can defend. Mixing the two can make it impossible to tell whether the proposed change addresses the right cause.

Choose one of your three friction points and ask what a minimal improvement could look like. It could be a required input field, an explicit reviewer, a clear assignment, a more reliable status notice or a modest AI assistant that organizes approved information.

Name the human owner of the improved step. State how success would be observed, such as fewer requests waiting for unspecified information, fewer returns for correction, or a dependable owner assigned before work begins. Do not claim a measured saving if no baseline has been collected.

A workflow becomes more trustworthy when another person can trace a request through it and identify who is responsible at every decision point.

### Chapter 4 — Verify the map and save a defensible improvement
Now you are ready to use your AI assistant as an organizing partner. In Journey One, you learned that AI can make weak information sound convincing. That risk still exists here. A model can invent a missing workflow step, assume that a system is connected, or present a common business practice as something your own team already does.

Give your chosen assistant only the process notes you are allowed to share. Ask it to organize those notes into people, inputs, steps, decisions, tools, handoffs and review. Require it to flag missing information and possible friction without inventing steps. This is the approved request you will find in both the lesson and workbook.

When the model returns a workflow, inspect every important step. Is each role supported by your source? Did it add an approval that does not occur? Did it omit the person who receives an incomplete request? Did it turn a possible cause into a proven one? Correct those errors before putting the map into the Project Record.

How you verify the map depends on the setting. You might trace a fictional example from start to finish, review an authorized process description, or ask a person who performs the task whether the map reflects real activity. Record how the check was done. A simulated test is valuable for learning but cannot be presented as live operational research.

Return to your three friction points. For one, write the evidence for its likely cause. If the cause is still unknown, state the next respectful and authorized observation or question that would resolve it. Choose a small improvement that removes or reduces a cause. Do not simply send the same delay to another department or automate a late step while ignoring a missing input.

Trace one complete request from entry to verified completion. Include its decision branches, the person who receives each handoff and the point at which a draft must be checked or withheld. Confirm how the proposed change would affect that trace.

Finally, append the current-state Workflow View, the three friction points, evidence labels, corrected AI output and one chosen improvement to the same continuing Project Record from Journey One. Retain your existing FOCUS capstone charter and Professional AI Operating Model. They will help you determine whether the new workflow is appropriately scoped, measurable and responsible.

In the next lesson, we will turn to communication that serves the audience. The workflow you mapped will show which message, report or explanation is genuinely needed, by whom, and at which moment.

This is how we build AI into real work: first make the process visible, then locate the cause of friction, preserve human decisions and test a change that could actually help.
