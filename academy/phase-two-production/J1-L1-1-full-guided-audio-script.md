## Segment 1 — Begin with the work you can stand behind

[Visual: a clean split screen. Left, a polished but unverified business report. Right, a smaller panel labeled “Source / Review / Decision.”]

Imagine you're responsible for sending a weekly update to a manager, a customer, or a colleague. You know what the update needs to accomplish. You have some notes and figures. You ask your AI assistant to organize those notes and help you draft the report.

A few moments later, the result looks polished. The headings are clear, the paragraphs flow naturally, and the conclusion sounds decisive.

But the appearance of a finished report is not the same as finished professional work.

Before you send it, there are questions only a person responsible for the task can answer. What exactly was the report intended to communicate? Which statements can you prove? Have you included information you are permitted to share? Who is relying on this result? If an important detail is wrong, who has responsibility for correcting it?

That is the starting point of professional AI judgment.

You are not learning to compete with AI at arranging sentences. You're learning to direct the work toward a real need and to recognize when the result is dependable.

Let's take a familiar situation. Someone working in customer service wants to answer a recurring question more consistently. An AI assistant could draft a response or arrange the instructions into a clearer order. But the professional task is not “have AI write an answer.” The task is “help the customer understand the correct next step, using current approved information.”

Notice the difference. One description focuses on the tool. The other identifies a person, a purpose, and a useful result.

That distinction travels with you across platforms. You could use a general AI assistant, a business system with built-in AI, or another tool your organization approves. You still need to know what result the work should produce.

[Pause for learner action, about 25 seconds.]

Think about a recurring task in your own work or a business you want to develop. Name the person it serves. Then finish this sentence: “A successful result would mean that this person can…”

Don't worry about the technology yet. Begin with the work and the person. That is where useful AI projects start.

## Segment 2 — Capability means evidence, not appearance

[Visual: two side-by-side examples. “I use AI for reports” compared with “I check important figures against the weekly record before approving an AI-assisted report.”]

Now let's consider the word capability.

Someone may say, “I know how to use AI for business.” That may be true, but it doesn't tell us what the person can reliably accomplish.

A professional capability is specific. You can describe the task, perform it, inspect the result, and explain why you chose the final action. Ideally, you can show a real example.

Consider two workers. Both ask an AI assistant to create a report from their notes. Both receive a useful-looking draft.

The first worker sends the report because it sounds professional.

The second worker checks the numbers against the source record, notices that one sentence makes a promise the team hasn't approved, and rewrites that sentence before the report is shared.

Both used an AI tool. Only the second example makes the human judgment visible.

The ability isn't simply to generate text. It includes recognizing what must be checked, knowing which evidence matters, and making a correction you can defend.

In today's project activity, you will identify five capabilities. Do not make these five names of AI products. Make them five actions you can demonstrate.

For example, one capability might be turning rough notes into a clear customer message. Another might be comparing alternatives against a stated set of requirements. Another might be identifying claims in a generated report that need independent evidence.

For each one, identify an example that supports your statement. You may have a sanitized work sample, a corrected draft, a comparison you performed, or a decision note that explains your reasoning.

If you cannot supply meaningful evidence yet, don't invent it. Record the gap.

You will also name two abilities or knowledge areas that still need development. A gap might be testing a workflow across more than one case. It might be learning what approvals are needed before a business process can be automated. It might be becoming more reliable at checking numerical claims.

Those are not failures. They are your honest starting points for professional development.

[Pause for learner action, about 30 seconds.]

Think of one thing you can already demonstrate, and name the evidence. Then think of one thing you cannot yet demonstrate. State what you would need to practice or verify before claiming that capability.

The professional difference is not confidence in the sound of your answer. It is confidence in what your evidence supports.

## Segment 3 — Four human responsibilities

[Visual: four connected stations, PURPOSE → CONTEXT → JUDGMENT → ACCOUNTABILITY. A separate AI-assistance lane supports the process without replacing the stations.]

Let's look at four responsibilities that stay with the person directing the work.

The first is purpose. What does this task need to accomplish, and who is it for? You can ask AI to help you clarify a purpose statement, but the tool doesn't decide whose interests your work must serve.

The second is context. What information, rules, permissions, and constraints define the task? A generated response can be fluent while missing an important fact. You must provide appropriate context and notice when essential information is missing.

The third is judgment. Does the result fit the purpose? Are the claims supported? Could it cause confusion or an avoidable problem? Judgment includes deciding not to use a result that has not earned your trust.

The fourth is accountability. Someone must be able to approve, correct, pause, or reject the work. That person might be a business owner, a manager, a designated reviewer, or you yourself when the decision is yours to make.

Now consider the same customer-service task again.

Purpose: give a correct, useful answer to a customer.

Context: the current approved company policy, the question the customer asked, and the boundaries of the service.

AI support: organize the available information and propose a clear response.

Human verification: compare the proposed response with the approved policy and check for missing or unsupported promises.

Decision owner: the person authorized to approve the message before it reaches the customer.

Stop condition: the policy is unclear, a customer-specific detail is sensitive, or the response promises something the company hasn't authorized.

This is a simple responsibility map. It is also the beginning of a professionally designed workflow.

When you later build more sophisticated automations or AI-assisted business systems, the complexity may increase. But the same questions will remain: What is the purpose? What does the system know? What checks are needed? Who can intervene? What should happen when the evidence is inadequate?

[Pause for learner action, about 30 seconds.]

Picture one recurring task in your own project. Say aloud or write down who makes the final decision. Then identify one condition that should pause the workflow rather than allowing it to continue automatically.

That gives you the beginning of an operating model: not just what AI does, but how a person remains in control.

## Segment 4 — Recognize over-delegation and prepare for the demonstration

[Visual: the fictional service report. Three verified labels are distinct from the unsupported “95% on time” claim.]

Now we are ready for our worked example.

In the fictional report we will examine, the source notes say that forty-two requests arrived and thirty-six were marked resolved.

The AI-assisted draft takes those notes and produces a confident sentence: ninety-five percent of requests were resolved on time.

Where did that percentage come from?

The notes do not give us the time each request took. They do not tell us whether a request met a deadline. So the on-time percentage is not established by the source.

That is an over-delegation risk: letting a convincing generated claim become a reported fact without the verification it requires.

The professional response isn't to blame the tool and abandon the task. The response is to check the evidence and correct the work.

We can retain the arrival count if the source supports it. We can retain the resolved count if we understand how “resolved” is defined. We cannot responsibly claim a ninety-five percent on-time result without a suitable timing record.

We also cannot claim the service improved without a verified comparison to an earlier period.

Notice how the review makes the report less dramatic but more trustworthy.

A dependable version might say: “The approved weekly record lists forty-two received requests and thirty-six marked resolved. Available notes do not establish the on-time percentage or whether performance improved over the previous period.”

That language shows what is known and what still needs investigation. It helps a decision-maker act on real information.

You will now use the visual demonstration in this lesson. Move through the source notes, the AI draft, the human review, and the corrected result.

Then return to your continuing project.

You will describe one recurring task, list five capabilities with evidence, identify two gaps, and create your first map of AI support and human responsibility.

Use the approved AI request with sanitized notes in your chosen assistant. Examine the output. If it invents a capability, assumes authority you don't have, or makes a claim you cannot verify, correct or reject that part.

Save one meaningful correction with a short reason.

[Pause for learner action, about 20 seconds.]

Before you continue, ask yourself one final question: If another person challenged the result tomorrow, could I explain where it came from and why I accepted it?

That is the confidence we are building: confidence grounded in skill, evidence, and responsibility.

Move on to the demonstration and your project activity. Your work today becomes the starting record for the next lesson, where we will test whether the need you're considering is worth solving.

AI assists. Humans verify.
