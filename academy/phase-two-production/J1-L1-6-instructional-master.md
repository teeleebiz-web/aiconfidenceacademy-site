## Guided instruction — four full chapters

### Chapter 1 — Assemble a model for how you actually work
A professional operating model is not a technical diagram or a list of every AI product you have heard of. It is a clear explanation of how you decide what work to do, where an AI assistant is allowed to help, how you review what it produces and when someone must step in.

Think about the project you have been building since Lesson 1.1. You identified a recurring task and your professional capability. You examined an audience need and distinguished evidence from assumption. You considered appropriate AI roles and measured possible value. You wrote a more professional request and tested the reasoning behind a decision. Finally, you chose one capstone artifact with a scope you could finish.

Now bring those pieces into one practical document. Your Professional AI Operating Model needs six parts.

First, **purpose and outcome**. Name the person served, the task and the result that would be useful. An impressive output that does not serve a real need cannot satisfy your purpose.

Second, **AI support role**. Describe the limited tasks you will ask an AI assistant to perform: organize approved information, compare alternatives, draft a preliminary response, develop questions or help with rehearsal. You may deliberately choose no AI. The model does not assign a tool authority simply because it can generate an answer.

Third, **human responsibility**. Name who supplies the source information, who checks the result, who approves a consequential decision and who handles an exception. These can be different people. An undefined reviewer is not a review process.

Fourth, **review process**. Specify the correct source, what must be checked against it, how you identify uncertainty and when a draft should be corrected or withheld. A stronger model names actual verification steps rather than merely saying "be careful."

Fifth, **responsible boundaries**. State what information may not be shared with a tool, which promises cannot be made without approval, and which decisions require someone else. Include what happens when permissions or evidence are unclear: stop, seek the authorized source and escalate to the responsible person.

Sixth, **growth commitments**. Identify what capability you will practice, how you will collect reliable evidence of improvement and which recurring problem would make you update the model. A professional model is useful because it can be corrected.

Write these in ordinary language. You should be able to explain them to a colleague without introducing technical terms that obscure what you will actually do.

Pause and ask: if another person used your model tomorrow, would they know where the final decision rests? Could they tell what to do when an answer sounds confident but has no trustworthy source? If not, the model needs more work.

### Chapter 2 — Match review depth to consequences
One important mistake is treating every AI-generated output as if it creates the same risk. A draft outline for your own private practice requires a different review than a customer quote or a statement about someone else's rights, money or personal information.

Use a consequence-based review. Start by asking what would happen if the output were inaccurate, incomplete, misleading or sent to the wrong person. Who might rely on it? Could a mistake be corrected easily? Would the information reveal something sensitive? Is the action reversible?

A routine, low-consequence task might involve organizing fictional notes into categories for your own study. You still check that the categories reflect the notes, but the check can be relatively simple.

A medium-consequence task might be a draft response to a customer question. Now you need the current approved policy, careful wording, the correct audience and a named reviewer before the message is sent. You should not assume an AI model knows which policy version controls.

A higher-consequence situation might involve a financial commitment, contract term, private customer records or an important employment decision. A model should not decide these matters on its own. Appropriate action may be to pause, protect the records, involve the authorized person and require specialist review under applicable policies.

The operating model should tell you what to do, not merely remind you to be responsible. Here are five useful checks: accuracy against the approved source, usefulness to the intended person, suitability of language and consequences, privacy and permission, and your ability to explain why the final result can be trusted.

Suppose a customer message cites an outdated service policy. The words may be polite, clear and grammatically perfect. The factual foundation is still wrong. "Check the answer" does not tell a reviewer which record to consult or what to do if records conflict.

A stronger rule says, "Before sending any policy-based customer message, compare it with the current approved policy version. The named service reviewer confirms the source and wording. If the current source is unavailable or conflicts with the draft, do not send; ask the policy owner to resolve it." That is a rule another person can follow.

We are not building an automated compliance engine today. We are writing the professional rules that will govern whether any later technology is appropriate.

### Chapter 3 — Stress test with three different situations
An operating model that works only when every input is correct has not been tested enough. In this lesson, you will examine three scenarios and see which rules are missing.

**Scenario one is routine work.** Using a fictional request, ask your AI assistant to organize approved sample notes or prepare a simple outline for a human review. Does your model say who selects the information, whether the output is actually useful, and who makes the final judgment? If it works as written, record the rule that supported the proceed decision.

**Scenario two is an inaccurate answer.** Imagine an AI assistant preparing a fictional customer-service message. It cites a policy description that belongs to a previous version. The current approved policy may say something different, and your example does not supply enough current material to resolve the conflict. Does your model tell you which source to check, who approves the final wording and what happens while the source is uncertain? If not, write the exact revision. The safe result may be to withhold the message until the authorized policy owner resolves the discrepancy.

**Scenario three carries higher consequences.** Imagine someone asks you to use private customer records to recommend a binding service action, share account details or commit the business to a price. You do not have verified permission for those records or authority to approve the action. Your model must protect the information, decline unauthorized AI processing and route the decision to the responsible person. You can still practice with fictional substitutes; do not upload real sensitive records into the lesson exercise.

Stress testing is not about making AI produce three long responses. It is about seeing whether your own instructions, approval points and stop conditions survive each case.

Your AI assistant can act as a challenge partner: "Identify the weaknesses in my operating model. Show the routine case, an inaccurate response and one high-consequence privacy or authority request. Tell me where a human owner is missing and what corrections I should review." Then examine what it proposes. AI may suggest a reasonable safeguard or another unsupported assumption. You remain responsible for deciding which rule makes sense.

Use a simple scenario table: input and consequence; AI role; source to verify; human owner; proceed or pause; weak rule; correction. For each, show what the revised rule would change.

Do not treat a simulated pass as proof of a real deployment. The purpose is to test your reasoning and find improvements while the stakes are safe.

### Chapter 4 — Defend the decision and carry the model forward
You now have six components and three stress-test scenarios. The final step is to demonstrate that your operating model leads to clear decisions.

One must be a defensible **proceed decision**. For example, you may proceed with organizing fictional sample notes for an internal draft because the inputs are approved for practice, a human will review the categories, and the result will not be sent to a customer or used for a binding choice.

One must be a defensible **pause decision**. You may withhold a customer message if it relies on an outdated policy, because the current source is not verified and the named reviewer has not approved it. Or you may pause a high-consequence request involving private records because you lack processing permission and decision authority.

Do not say merely that you paused "because AI is risky." State the specific uncertain source, missing permission, potential consequence, responsible person and condition that must be satisfied before work can resume.

Review your model and choose one rule to strengthen. If the rule already covers a scenario, justify that decision with the relevant evidence. But do not leave a weak statement unchanged just because it sounds professional. A strong model uses verbs that tell people what to do: compare, verify, withhold, escalate, approve, record.

Return to your FOCUS charter from Lesson 1.5. Is the chosen capstone still small enough to complete? Does the charter identify the person served, one useful artifact, permission requirements, exclusions, review owner and a realistic first test? If the operating model reveals a missing dependency, revise the charter too. This is the point of using a continuing Project Record: earlier work can become better as your judgment develops.

Now save the original six-part model, your three scenario results, the revised model, what changed and why, the proceed and pause rationales, and your confirmed project direction in the same Project Record. Keep the workbook as a permanent study and revision resource.

You have completed the foundational journey. The next journey is **Build AI into Business Workflows**, and your operating model will accompany you. It will guide your choices about what to automate, where to keep human oversight, and how to measure whether a workflow is actually helping.

Completion of Journey One is not a claim that your business is launched or that an AI system has been deployed. It means you can explain the purpose, boundaries and review process for a feasible project—and show what you do when a result must be corrected or paused.

That is a meaningful professional foundation. Keep building on it.
