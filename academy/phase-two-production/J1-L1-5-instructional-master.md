## Guided instruction — four full chapters

### Chapter 1 — A solution concept is not permission to deploy
Welcome to the point where your investigation begins to become a project. In Lesson 1.2, you looked for a person with a need rather than a product to sell. In Lesson 1.3, you considered appropriate roles for AI and what business value would mean. In Lesson 1.4, you improved professional requests and recorded a provisional decision. The work you saved already gives you a stronger starting point than a generic idea.

Today, the goal is a finishable piece of work. That may mean a sample business artifact, a demonstration based on fictional inputs, or an approved workflow proposal. It does not have to mean launching an entire business or deploying AI in a client's systems.

Begin with a solution concept. In one paragraph, describe the problem, the people affected, the desired outcome, the current workflow, the narrow AI role if appropriate, the human owner, the information the artifact would need, the possible value, the risks and limits, and the question your test must answer.

These fields matter because a proposal can skip important realities. Imagine a business owner says, "I want an AI assistant to do customer proposals." What kind of proposals? Based on which approved information? Who will review price commitments? Will it communicate with real customers? How much information has actually been provided? Without those distinctions, you don't have a project that another person can safely approve or evaluate.

A responsible starting point might be much smaller: a demonstration assistant that drafts one kind of estimate using approved fictional examples. A human checks the source of every description, figure and promise. No contract is accepted, no payment is collected, and no customer-facing message is sent automatically. This is a testable artifact, not an authorized commercial deployment.

One important phrase belongs in your notes: a solution concept is a possibility, not permission. It should make the next decision easier, not conceal a missing approval.

Consider your own project. What person is served? What result could you show in a short, honest demonstration? What must stay with an authorized person? Write your first answer before asking an AI assistant to expand it.

### Chapter 2 — Use FOCUS to define the capstone
The Academy's FOCUS Capstone Frame is a method for turning broad intentions into work that can be finished and evaluated. Here FOCUS stands for Function, Owner, Customer, Use Case and Scope.

Function describes what the artifact actually does. Not "transform business operations," but "prepare a draft service estimate from an approved sample request." The clearer the function, the easier it is to test.

Owner identifies the person accountable for reviewing and approving what the artifact produces. If you are creating a personal practice demonstration, you may own its review. If an artifact would affect an employer or client, the accountable decision maker may be somebody else. AI does not replace that person.

Customer identifies who would use or benefit from the work. It could be an internal team member, a small-business owner, a prospective customer or a learner. A fictional user in a training example must be labeled fictional. Do not assume that identifying a likely customer proves willingness to pay.

Use Case describes the situation in which the artifact will be used, including inputs and the result needed. For example: "Given a fictional request and an approved list of sample service descriptions, draft an estimate for human review." That can be rehearsed without exposing private information.

Scope makes the project finishable. It describes what the first version includes, excludes and depends upon. A small artifact could include one input form, one prompt template and a human review checklist. It might explicitly exclude external integrations, live customer data, billing, contracts and automatic approvals.

An artifact may have one impressive feature and still be incomplete if it has no reliable way to check its output. Conversely, a simple, well-contained demonstration can be complete when the expected input, output, review and limits all work.

Use FOCUS to test the shape of the project you started in Lesson 1.1. If you cannot describe the function in one sentence, narrow it. If no one has responsibility for the result, name an owner. If the beneficiary is vague, return to the evidence from Lesson 1.2. If the use case requires permissions or data you do not have, redesign the example using authorized or fictional material. If the scope still contains five different systems, reduce it to one complete artifact.

At this stage, good judgment is often the ability to say, "Not yet."

### Chapter 3 — Compare three artifacts and select one
A capstone does not become more valuable merely because it contains more functions. We want you to finish an artifact that demonstrates judgment, useful AI direction, verification and appropriate human control.

For our fictional proposal business, three candidates might be considered. The first is an approved reference guide that helps someone locate current service instructions. The second is a draft estimate generator that uses fictional requests and approved sample descriptions. The third is a complete AI customer-service system connected to live messages, invoicing and payments.

We can compare feasibility, usefulness and consequences without inventing customer demand. The reference guide could be relatively easy to prepare and verify if the information is already approved, but it may not exercise much AI assistance. The sample estimate generator might demonstrate a narrow AI role, a reusable prompt and a clear human check. The full customer-service system would involve new permissions, integrations, sensitive information and many more failure points. It is unlikely to be a sensible first capstone without a much larger authorized team and budget.

We should not declare any of these options profitable. The case is fictional, and no actual customer research or implementation-cost evidence was provided. Instead, ask which artifact the learner can realistically build and demonstrate with the tools and permissions available.

Set clear success criteria before building. For example, the draft estimate generator could pass if it accepts a fictional request, produces a draft using only supplied sample information, explicitly flags missing details, and never presents an unsupported price or promise as approved. A responsible reviewer would inspect its output.

Then establish exclusions. It will not sign contracts. It will not collect payments. It will not determine binding quotes from missing cost data. It will not send messages to real customers. Those are not flaws in the capstone. They are boundaries that help make it finishable and responsible.

Now consider three artifact candidates for your actual project. Compare the time required, the tools you can use, the approved information you have, the value that can be demonstrated, the consequences of a wrong answer and the human oversight needed. Choose one candidate and write why the other two will wait.

A finished small artifact can provide stronger evidence of professional capability than a much larger plan that never becomes testable.

### Chapter 4 — Write the charter and prepare the first test
You are ready to write a compact project charter. A charter is not a sales pitch. It is a clear agreement with yourself and, where relevant, with the people whose approval will be required.

Begin with the affected person and need, as established in Lesson 1.2. Record the intended result and the measure of usefulness from Lesson 1.3. State the human decision owner and the constraints from Lesson 1.4. Add your selected artifact, its function, input, output, review standard and limits.

Keep the charter short enough that a reviewer can understand what will be delivered. A useful version might say: "I will create a demonstration that prepares a draft service estimate from an approved set of fictional examples. It will identify missing details and ask a human to review all figures and promises. Success means the sample can be completed and checked without inventing customer commitments. The artifact will not approve a price, make a contract, charge a customer or access live systems."

That statement is narrow enough to guide construction and testing. It still does not prove anyone will purchase the service. That would require separate evidence.

Your AI assistant can help inspect the charter. Ask it to apply Function, Owner, Customer, Use Case and Scope. Ask what is missing, what assumption may be unsupported, and whether a smaller completed artifact would serve the learning goal better. Explicitly instruct it not to assume permission to deploy.

When you review the AI's feedback, consider your actual time, access to tools and authority. Eliminate dependencies that exist only in the model's imagination. Do not promise an integration you cannot test. Do not call a simulated success an approved customer result.

Choose one realistic first test. Define the sample input, expected output, human review step and the condition that would count as a failure. You may test with safe fictional material on your own device. If the project depends on private employer data or client approval, the first step may be to ask permission rather than send the information to AI.

Save your charter, three alternatives and reason for selection, compact solution concept, success criteria, exclusions, permission dependencies and first test in the same Project Record. Your Lesson 1.5 workbook remains available for future study even after the teaching window closes.

This is a meaningful transition in the journey. You have moved from a broad interest in AI to a defined professional task, an evidence-based need, a measurable value hypothesis, responsible requests and now a realistic artifact.

Your success is not measured by how complicated your plan sounds. It is measured by whether you can finish something useful, test it and explain where human responsibility begins and ends.
