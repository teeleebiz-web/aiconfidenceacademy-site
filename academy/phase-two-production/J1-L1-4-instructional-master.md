## Instructor introduction — recording script

Welcome back to the AI Confidence Academy. You have identified a need worth investigating, examined where AI may help, and learned to measure business value rather than simply counting outputs. Today we will bring those decisions into a professional conversation with AI.

An AI assistant can produce an attractive recommendation in seconds. But if you have not supplied the purpose, the person it serves, the necessary facts, and the limitations, that recommendation may only sound convincing.

In this lesson you will learn a Professional Request Framework built around six essentials: purpose, audience, context, task, constraints and quality. Then you will ask your own AI assistant to compare two options, challenge an unsupported assumption, and decide what evidence you still need.

Your final responsibility is not to accept the recommendation. It is to make a decision you can explain and defend. We will practice with a fictional founder who has a promising business idea, but not yet enough facts to choose a service. Let's begin.

## Guided instruction — four full chapters

### Chapter 1 — Build a request that reflects professional work
Consider a simple question: "What service should I offer?" It sounds reasonable. Yet an AI assistant cannot know which customer you intend to serve, whether you have permission to use particular information, what you can deliver, or which option has actual demand. It may respond confidently with an attractive list. That is not the same as a dependable choice.

A professional request begins with purpose. Why does the work matter? It identifies an audience. Who will use or rely on the result? It includes context. What relevant evidence and constraints are already known? It states the task. What should the AI assistant actually produce? It specifies constraints, including privacy, authority, approved sources, budget or delivery limits. And it states quality: how will you judge whether the answer is accurate and useful?

These are six components of the Professional Request Framework: purpose, audience, context, task, constraints and quality. You can use them with ChatGPT or another AI assistant. They are not magic words. They are a disciplined way to make your responsibility visible.

Suppose our fictional founder is considering two services for a small customer-service business. The founder has noticed recurring questions, but has not performed customer interviews or measured the time and expense of delivery. One option is preparing an approved information guide. The other is a custom AI response assistant.

A vague request might say, "Which option will make more money?" A professional request would ask for a comparison of the two approaches, using the need statement from the founder's notes, without treating hypothetical demand as fact. It would require the assistant to identify missing customer evidence, delivery capacity and costs before giving a confident recommendation.

Notice the difference. The professional request may lead to a less dramatic answer. It may say, "These facts are not established." That is often more valuable than an unsupported sales forecast.

Return to your continuing project. Bring forward the person served, the need you selected, and the AI role or no-AI choice from the earlier lessons. The work does not begin again. We improve its direction.

Pause and identify one important context item you have not yet supplied. Is it authorization, a customer requirement, a measured baseline or a delivery limit? A good question can expose the next responsible action.

### Chapter 2 — Ask for clarification before a recommendation
AI is often rewarded by the appearance of answering quickly. Professional work sometimes requires a more useful first response: a clarifying question.

Suppose you ask whether to offer an approved reference guide or a bespoke AI assistant. Perhaps you have a clear audience and a description of the current problem. But you do not know how many people experience it, what software the client already uses, whether their policies are current, what the client can pay, or who has authority to approve responses.

An AI assistant can help list those gaps. What it cannot do is convert them into facts by inventing plausible numbers. An estimate or hypothesis may help structure a test if it is labeled as such. It must never be presented as an actual customer interview, approved policy, confirmed cost or forecast.

In your request, tell the assistant: Separate supplied facts, inferred assumptions and unverified suggestions. Ask clarifying questions when important evidence is missing. If a conclusion depends on an unknown, describe the uncertainty and what would resolve it.

Let's practice a distinction. "Three authorized staff members reported that customers ask similar questions" would be a reported experience, if those conversations actually occurred. "The approved service policy was last updated on a certain date" would require the policy record. "Customers will choose the chatbot" is an unsupported preference claim unless the founder has evidence. The wording of the AI response does not change the category of its source.

When you review an AI response, locate the statements that need verification. Which come from your own notes? Which are reasonable possibilities but not yet supported? Which are outright invented? The goal is not to find fault in every sentence. The goal is to know which facts must be dependable before you act.

One useful professional follow-up is not "Make this more persuasive." It is "What important input is missing, and how could I check it without leading the person toward a particular answer?" That question moves the investigation forward.

Now return to the original request you wrote. Add one explicit instruction requiring clarification before a recommendation if a material fact is absent. Think about which missing input could reverse the decision, not merely delay the presentation.

### Chapter 3 — Use AI to compare alternatives without handing it authority
A professional AI assistant can organize information, compare alternatives and reveal tradeoffs. But the decision still belongs to the person authorized to make it.

Imagine Option A: prepare and maintain a concise approved reference guide. Option B: introduce an AI-supported response assistant. We can examine their possible usefulness, required inputs, human oversight and unresolved costs. A guide might be simpler to deliver, but its effectiveness depends on accurate current instructions and whether people can find them. An AI response assistant may help organize and retrieve information, but it adds testing, governance, review and maintenance requirements.

We should not declare either the winner from a fictional example. We lack measured demand, full implementation costs, client permissions and actual results. The absence of those facts must be visible in the comparison.

A good comparison separates alternatives, the evidence favoring each, material tradeoffs, unresolved assumptions, and consequences of being wrong. It also identifies the accountable decision maker. That way, AI helps clarify the choice without claiming authority to make it.

Consider the difference between two follow-up requests. "Choose the best option" invites AI to fill gaps with an answer. "For each option, list what we know, what we have only assumed, the required review effort, the principal risk, and the one question that would most change the choice" produces a more usable decision aid.

The aim is not to keep prompting until you get the answer you hoped to hear. It is to improve the quality of the decision. If the assistant recommends a customer-facing AI system but your current evidence cannot establish approved source information, your responsible next move may be to validate the source first.

Write down a material claim in your comparison. Perhaps it concerns delivery time, demand, reduced corrections or savings. State the source you would use to verify it. If the source is not available, mark the claim as unverified and remove it from the final rationale.

This is how the Human First principle becomes practical. AI assists with examination, but the accountable person retains judgment.

### Chapter 4 — Create a decision memo and preserve the conversation
A decision memo is a brief record of your reasoning, not a polished essay designed to impress.

It should name the decision in plain language. List the alternatives. Summarize the evidence actually supplied. Explain the important tradeoffs and uncertainties. State which information still needs verification. Identify the person with authority to decide and the reason for the provisional choice.

In our fictional founder example, a responsible memo might say that the approved reference guide merits a small first test because the business can evaluate it without committing to an unverified automated service. But that remains a provisional choice, not a claim that customer demand or profitability has already been established. The decision could change after a genuine review of customer needs, approved materials, delivery capacity and costs.

Now you will practice this process with your own selected project. Save your original AI request, even if it was vague. Improve it using all six elements of the Professional Request Framework. Ask your chosen AI assistant to compare Option A and Option B. Review what it returned. Challenge one assumption and verify or clearly flag one material claim. Ask a follow-up that closes a concrete gap in the decision.

Look carefully at the outcome. Did your follow-up provide missing evidence, request a relevant clarification, or improve the comparison? Or did it merely produce more text? A longer output is not necessarily a better decision.

Save the original request, the improved request, your substantive follow-up, the corrected or unresolved claim, and a brief decision memo in the same continuing Project Record. Preserve the evidence from Lessons 1.1, 1.2 and 1.3. Your workbook is your permanent study resource, not a replacement for your project.

You may use AI to draft the memo for your own review. But you, not the model, approve the reasoning and the final action. Name any decision that requires another person's permission. Do not imply that a simulated exercise gives you authority to deploy a service.

To close, return to the six elements: purpose, audience, context, task, constraints and quality. Then ask the deeper question: does this exchange support a reliable professional judgment? That is the standard we're building, one careful decision at a time.

## Applied AI workshop — 25 minutes

Learners use an AI assistant of their own choice on phone, tablet or computer; no private or unauthorized customer data.
1. Bring forward the person, need, evidence, AI role, no-AI alternative and provisional value measure from Lessons 1.1–1.3.
2. Record an initial request for the existing project and improve it using the six elements of the Professional Request Framework.
3. Copy the approved prompt to the chosen assistant, comparing two project-relevant options. Review its claim categories: supplied evidence / inference / unverified suggestion.
4. Challenge one unsupported assumption; identify the material claim and authorized source to verify it. If evidence is unavailable, clearly mark it unresolved.
5. Ask one clarifying follow-up that changes the decision's usefulness rather than merely the length of the response.
6. Save the two requests, follow-up, material-claim review, comparison, human decision owner and a brief decision memo in the continuing Project Record. Append; never overwrite earlier evidence.
